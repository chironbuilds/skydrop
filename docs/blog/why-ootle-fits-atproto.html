The gap in the Atmosphere

The atproto community has a clear story about identity and data. An account's permanent ID is a decentralized identifier (DID), and its handle is a domain name that can change. Its likes, follows and posts are records in a public per-user database on a Personal Data Server (PDS). Lexicons name the schemas, relays carry the firehose, and App Views aggregate it. Dan Abramov sums up the movement as: “What open source did for code, open social does for data.”

Money is missing from that story. The Atmosphere can prove that you wrote something, and it has no native way to pay you for it. Creators can't be tipped, and the people running feeds and labelers have no protocol-level way to get paid. Anyone who wants to reward an account has to bolt on a payment company, and that company becomes the kind of gatekeeper the protocol was designed to route around.

So I started from a question. If you took atproto's principles seriously, what would you require of a money layer? I built Skydrop to find out. It pays Bluesky accounts for verifiable actions, on Tari's Ootle network. Each section below is one requirement I arrived at, and how Ootle met it.

Self-authenticating data should stay that way

atproto's docs say “all data is signed”, which is why an app can trust a record it received from a relay. The usual way to connect a social network to a chain throws that away. A server watches the network and attests to what it saw, so the chain ends up trusting the server. The data was verifiable, and now it's hearsay.

The requirement is that the money layer must read atproto's proofs itself. It has to walk from a DID through its identity log, to a signed commit, down a Merkle search tree, to one record.

Ootle runs contracts as WebAssembly, which makes this straightforward. The verifier is one Rust library, and the same code runs in the browser and the contract. That matters more than it sounds, because it matches how atproto apps already work. An App View checks signatures and then shows you something. Here the page runs the same checks and shows you what you qualify for, and the contract runs them again and pays. There's one definition of "valid" across both.

Since all of this data is public, someone could copy your proof. So the claimant publishes one more ordinary record, a post containing a code tied to their wallet. It uses atproto's own primitive to answer "which wallet did this account choose?", and it needs no new signing scheme or browser plugin on the Bluesky side.

Credible exit has to cover the money

Credible exit is atproto's signature idea: account portability means a host or app can't hold you hostage. A money layer can undo that if it ties your funds to one app, one host or one company's database.

Three properties keep the exit open here:

  • Rewards follow the DID. A claim names a DID, so it makes no difference which PDS hosts the account or which app wrote the record. Move host, and the same identity keeps claiming.
  • Nobody owns the contracts. The deployed Campaign contract and link registry have no owner. No key exists that can change their rules, and that includes mine. A campaign's funder can only take back what's left after it closes.
  • Apps are replaceable. Abramov calls apps “many projections of the same data”, and a contract fits that description. It's one more reader of the same repositories, except this reader holds funds. Skydrop's website could vanish tomorrow, and anyone could build another client against the same public contracts.

There's also a nice symmetry in naming. Ootle addresses are long strings no friend would recognise, and atproto already solved that problem with handles. A wallet link is a single signed post, and after that a handle works like a payment address. atproto gives Ootle human names, and Ootle gives those names a purse.

Public speech needs private money

This is the requirement I care about most, and the one a typical transparent chain fails. atproto repositories are public by design. If you link a public identity to a fully transparent ledger, anyone can look up your handle and read your whole financial history: your balance, who pays you, and what you spend it on. Attaching that to a social network where strangers already argue with you is a safety problem.

Ootle wallets can hold funds in stealth outputs and reveal only what a given transaction needs. So the things you did in public stay public, as atproto intends, while your finances don't automatically join them. A campaign's pool and each claim are visible, so this isn't total privacy, and a linked wallet is publicly tied to its handle. But public speech and private money are a much better fit for a social network than public speech and public money.

Big world, per-user state

atproto calls itself “big-world, open social”. Everyone's data lives in their own repository, and one person's activity doesn't weigh down anyone else's. A money layer with one big shared state would be the wrong shape: a popular campaign would slow down or raise costs for everybody else.

Ootle's state is made of independent pieces, so the contracts could mirror that shape. Each campaign is its own component. Each claimant gets a receipt of their own, and a second claim fails because that receipt already exists. Across five claims, the campaign's shared state grew from 358 to 362 bytes. In a world where anyone can mint an identity for free, a shared list of users would be an invitation to bloat it. This design doesn't have one.

Social value is small value

Social actions are worth very little each. A tip might be a few cents, and a reward for following someone even less. If checking a proof costs more than the reward, the whole idea stays a demo. Verifying atproto's signatures and tree paths is real work, so this was the requirement I was least sure of.

These are testnet numbers, and tTARI has no value, so read them as proportions. A one-step claim on a real account came to 0.031–0.040 tTARI. That leaves room for small rewards, and the verification is happening on-chain. Only real usage on a real network will settle what those fees are worth, and I'd be careful about reading more into them than that.

What this could unlock

Skydrop's campaigns are the first use. The same pieces apply to problems the Atmosphere talks about a lot:

Atmosphere problemWhat a money layer addsStatus
Rewarding people for participatingCampaigns paying for likes, follows, posts or a vouch from a trusted accountLive on testnet
Fair airdrops to a communityPay a fixed list of DIDs, or the members of a Bluesky listLists live on testnet
Tipping by handleA signed post like "@bot tip @someone 5" spends a budget, with no app in controlBuilt and tested, not on the current network
Paying for feeds, labelers and other servicesatproto's service-auth tokens become metered, per-call paymentsBuilt and tested, not on the current network
Recovering a lost walletFriends approve a recovery by posting from their own accountsBuilt and tested, not on the current network

That second-to-last row is the one I'd most like to see someone pick up. The Atmosphere's infrastructure needs funding, and service-auth already identifies who's calling. Today the contracts handle Bluesky's record types, but the verifier itself works on any collection, so records from other Lexicons could become conditions as those apps grow.

Where it falls short

"Perfect" is my enthusiasm talking. These are the real limits, and most of them come from atproto's trust model, so no money layer can remove them.

  • Bots can farm it. A contract checks signatures, and it can't tell whether a person is behind them. In my tests a script with a self-made identity passed every claimant step without any Bluesky account. Open campaigns get farmed down to about the fee. Vouches from a trusted account or fixed lists are the defence.
  • Proofs don't cover time or deletion. Accounts sign their own timestamps, and a proof shows a record existed in one commit. It can't show the record hasn't been deleted since.
  • Your host can act as you. A PDS usually holds its accounts' signing keys and often a rotation key. One analysis notes this also weakens credible exit. If your host signs something, a contract sees a valid signature.
  • Key history is hard. A contract can't be certain it has seen an account's full key history. A recent audit found real problems here. The fixes are deployed on the testnet, but accounts still need someone observing them, especially before they've been seen for the first time.
  • It's a testnet. The token has no value, no outside auditor has read these contracts, and the full flow hasn't yet been run end to end with a real wallet and a real account.

Why I still say it fits

I haven't built this on every chain, so I can't say nothing else could work. But go back through the list: verify proofs natively, keep credible exit intact, keep money private when speech is public, match the per-user shape, and stay cheap enough for small values. A money layer for atproto has to do all five, and on Ootle I could get all five at once.

atproto signed its data so credible exit would be real. That same decision lets a contract act as one more projection in the Atmosphere, and I doubt that was anyone's plan.
